# Procedural Byts: a history

## 1970: CS1

*1970s heavy metal. Software development fundamentals.*

CS1 is the 1970 debut from Procedural Byts: twelve tracks of raw proto-metal covering a first-semester computer science course, from Hello, World to recursion. Nyble sings lead and plays bass, Byt plays lead guitar, and Bytes plays drums and sings backup. Every song teaches one concept, from variables and loops to arrays, objects, debugging and sorting, and the solos replay each lesson so you can hear it branch, loop, call and return. The band formed in a university basement lab at midnight after explaining their bugs to a rubber duck named Null. Play loud. Study louder.

### Where It All Began

Procedural Byts formed at exactly 00:00:00 UTC on January 1, 1970, which is why every Unix machine on Earth still counts time from the moment they met. Nobody has been able to confirm this. Nobody has disproved it either. What everyone agrees on is that the duck was there first.

They met in the basement computer lab of a university engineering building, the only room on campus that was loud enough, cold enough and empty enough after midnight. Nyble was there re-sorting a box of punch cards she had dropped down the stairs, using the only algorithm she knew at the time, which was crying. Byt was there because his program had been running since Tuesday and he was afraid that if he stopped it, he’d never find out what it did. Bytes was simply there. Nobody remembers him enrolling; he was allocated to the lab one night and never freed.

On top of the corner terminal sat a small yellow rubber duck. Nobody knew who owned it. Around 3 a.m., Nyble started explaining her card problem to it out loud, line by line. Two terminals over, Byt started explaining his runaway program to it too. Then Bytes joined in, mostly to have something to do. By sunrise, each of them had overheard the others’ bugs and fixed them, and the duck had said nothing at all. They agreed it was the best collaborator any of them had ever worked with.

They named the duck Null, because it never returned anything.

They didn’t have a garage, so they rehearsed in the machine room, where the mainframe’s cooling fans gave them their first sound engineer. Null sat on top of the mainframe, which by the band’s reasoning made it root. Their first song was “Hello, World,” because it was the only one they could all get to compile, and only after explaining it to the duck twice.

The band name started as a typo on the first flyer. Nyble meant to write “Procedural Bits,” after the step-by-step way they wrote every program, and it came out “Procedural Byts.” Byt filed it as a bug; Nyble closed the ticket as “working as intended.” Bytes argued that if the band was named after anyone it should be him, since he had more bits. They still argue about it, which is why every rehearsal starts with a merge conflict and ends with all three of them explaining their side to Null.

Their first gig was the department holiday party. Bytes counted the band in with “zero, one, two, three,” Nyble and Byt came in on four, and the whole set ran exactly one beat apart. The crowd loved it, the band called it off-by-one, and it has been their signature ever since. They were asked to leave when the encore entered an infinite loop and campus security had to pull the plug. Null was the only one in the room who didn’t panic.

Null still tours with them, riding on Bytes’s bass drum. Before every show, the band takes turns walking the duck through the set list, song by song, and they haven’t had a bad show since. They have been told many times that a rubber duck cannot be a member of a band. Nyble’s answer is always the same: if it looks like a band member and quacks like a band member, it’s a band member. Byt calls this duck typing. The duck has not commented.

## 1973: Fetch, Decode, Execute

*1973 heavy blues rock. Architecture and organization.*

Fetch, Decode, Execute is the 1973 second album from Procedural Byts: twelve tracks of heavy blues rock that open up the computer and look inside. Nyble sings lead and plays bass, Byt plays lead guitar, and Bytes plays drums and sings backup. Logic gates, binary and two’s complement, floating point, flip-flops, registers, the instruction cycle, machine code, the memory hierarchy, interrupts, pipelining and performance, all the way down to the stored-program idea that makes it all work. Recorded inside a mainframe cabinet for the reverb. Bytes found out he’s made of eight flip-flops. Play loud. Fetch, decode, repeat.

### Recorded Inside the Machine

After CS1, the band wanted to know what was underneath the code. Byt said it was magic. Nyble said it was gates. Null said nothing, which everyone took as a vote for gates. So they decided to record the second album inside the machine to find out.

They booked a weekend in the university’s machine room and set up inside an empty mainframe cabinet, “for the reverb.” The cooling fans are audible on every track. The engineer set the tempo of each song from the computer’s clock crystal, and Bytes refused to follow it, right up until the Flip-Flop sessions, when he learned he is made of eight flip-flops and accepted the clock as his true drummer.

Nyble finally looked up what a nibble is: four bits, half a byte, exactly one hexadecimal digit. She put it in the bridge of Two’s Complement and has been insufferable about it ever since.

Null spent the sessions sitting on the computer’s front panel. During one take of Speak in Opcodes, the duck settled onto the halt switch and stopped the machine dead. The band kept the take. It is the last bar of the bridge.

The cover is a photograph of that front panel. Eight of the lights are lit in the pattern 01001000, which is the letter H in ASCII, the first letter of Hello, World. The band says they meant to photograph the whole word, but the duck sat on the switches.

## 1976: Kernel Panic

*1976 hard rock with an early punk edge. Operating systems.*

Kernel Panic is the 1976 third album from Procedural Byts: twelve fast, raw tracks of hard rock with an early punk edge about the program that runs all the other programs. Nyble sings lead and plays bass, Byt plays lead guitar, and Bytes plays drums and sings backup. Booting, system calls, processes and fork, scheduling, context switches, locks, deadlock, virtual memory, page faults, file systems, device drivers and the crash that takes everything down. The recording sessions deadlocked for three weeks until someone moved the duck. Play loud. Then reboot.

### The Deadlock Sessions

By 1976 the band had played enough shows to have strong opinions about who should go first, who should wait, and who was hogging the microphone. So they wrote an album about the program that settles those arguments for every computer: the operating system.

The studio had one good microphone, one good amplifier and one good pair of drumsticks. Within a week, Nyble had the microphone and was waiting for Byt to finish his guitar part, Byt had the amp and was waiting for Bytes to finish the drums, and Bytes had the sticks and was waiting for Nyble’s lyrics. Nobody moved for three weeks.

The deadlock ended when the producer picked up Null, who had been sitting on the only copy of the lyric sheet the whole time, and everyone realized they could start again. Deadlock (Nobody Moves) was written that afternoon.

After that, the producer ran the sessions round robin: four hours each, then out, ready or not. Byt complained that he was being starved. Bytes pointed out that the real problem was the zombie.

The zombie was Byt’s fault. He had tried to fork himself so he could play rhythm and lead at the same time. The child process hung around the studio for days, eating everyone’s sandwiches, until Nyble finally reaped it.

On the last day, the tape machine crashed hard. The band recorded the title track in one take while it rebooted, which is why the album ends the way it does.

## 1979: Syntax Error

*1979 new wave of British heavy metal. Foundations of programming languages.*

Syntax Error is the 1979 fourth album from Procedural Byts: twelve tracks of new wave of British heavy metal about programming languages themselves. Nyble sings lead and plays bass, Byt plays lead guitar, and Bytes plays drums and sings backup. Side A covers how code is read and run: lexers, parsers, compilers and interpreters, types, scope and garbage collection. Side B is functional programming, and it has no guitar, because Byt refused to play songs with no side effects. Lambdas, pure functions, immutability, map, filter, reduce and closures, all driven by fuzz bass and drums. Byt comes back for the closer, Paradigm Shift. Play loud. Parse carefully.

### Side B Has No Guitar

In 1979 the band set out to make an album about programming languages themselves: how code gets read, checked, translated and run, and the different ways of thinking that languages give you.

Side A went smoothly. Lexers, parsers, compilers, types and scope, and a garbage collector Bytes described as “the roadie we always needed.”

Side B was going to be functional programming. Byt read the lyric sheet for No Side Effects, put his guitar back in its case, and announced that he would not play on any song that didn’t change the world. Nyble pointed out that functional programs do change the world, just at the edges. Byt said the edges were exactly where he would be standing.

So Side B has no guitar. Nyble played lead on bass through a fuzz pedal, and Bytes played the drums like they owed him money. Reviewers called it a bold minimalist turn. Byt called it a principled stand. Null sat next to him on the studio steps the whole time, in solidarity or by coincidence.

Byt came back for the final track, Paradigm Shift, on the grounds that a paradigm shift is the biggest side effect there is. Nobody argued.

The title came from the first test pressing, which skipped so badly the factory stamped SYNTAX ERROR on the sleeve as a warning. The band liked it better than their own title, which nobody can remember.

## 1982: Packet Storm

*1982 classic heavy metal. Networking and communication.*

Packet Storm is the 1982 fifth album from Procedural Byts: twelve tracks of classic heavy metal about how computers talk to each other. Nyble sings lead and plays bass, Byt plays lead guitar, and Bytes plays drums and sings backup. Packet switching, the seven layers, Ethernet collisions, addresses and ports, routing, the three-way handshake, sequence numbers, packet loss, UDP, congestion control, name servers, and the day the whole network switched to TCP/IP at once. Recorded in three different cities and sent by mail, one reel at a time. Some arrived out of order. One never arrived. Play loud. Acknowledge receipt.

### Recorded by Mail

After Syntax Error, the band spent 1981 in three different cities. Byt was still not discussing Side B. So when it came time to make an album about networks, they decided to record it over one: the postal service.

Each member recorded their parts at home and mailed the reels to the studio. Before any tape could be sent, Nyble insisted on a handshake: a letter asking “ready to receive?”, a reply saying “ready, are you?”, and a third letter saying “sending.” It took six weeks before a single note crossed the country.

Bytes’s drum reels arrived out of order, so the engineer numbered every reel and reassembled them by sequence number. Two reels arrived twice and were thrown out. One never arrived at all, and after three weeks with no word, Bytes recorded it again. That became Packet Loss.

Byt’s tapes went through four post offices on the way to the studio, one of them in the wrong country. He wrote Router of the Lost while he waited.

Null traveled in the box with the master tapes as packing material, there and back, on every single shipment. It is the most-traveled duck in rock.

The closer, Flag Day, was written for January 1, 1983, when the whole network switched to TCP/IP at the same moment. The band was finally back in one room to record it, which Nyble says was the real flag day.

## 1985: CS2: Live on the Abstract Data Tour ’85

*1985 heavy metal. Algorithmic foundations.*

CS2: Live on the Abstract Data Tour ’85 is the first live album from Procedural Byts: twelve tracks of 1985 arena metal covering a second-semester computer science course, recorded in front of roaring crowds from Madison Square Garbage Collection to Log Angeles. Nyble sings lead and plays bass, Byt plays lead guitar, Bytes plays drums and sings backup, and new member Wyrd brings a wall of synthesizers and a keytar. Inheritance, polymorphism, exceptions, generics, linked lists, stacks, queues, hash tables, trees, sorting and Big-O, with the crowd shouting back every answer. The tour bus was a linked list, so they couldn’t go back for Pittsburgh. The duck rode on top of the synth, which made it root. Play loud. Push, pop, repeat.

### The Tour

By 1985, eight bits wasn’t enough. Byt placed an ad in the back of a computer magazine: “Metal band seeks wider member. Must own synth. Must align on word boundaries.” Wyrd answered by modem at 300 baud. The reply took four days to arrive and said only “yes,” padded with zeros out to thirty-two bits.

The first rehearsal was rough. Wyrd tried to play everything at once, and Bytes complained that the new member was taking up four of his slots. Then Wyrd sat down across from Null and explained the problem out loud, line by line. The duck said nothing. Wyrd was in. Nyble checked the paperwork afterwards: Wyrd implemented every method in the BandMember interface, so as far as she was concerned, it compiled.

They toured in a bus they named the Linked List, because every stop only knew where the next one was. Halfway through the tour they drove past Pittsburgh by mistake and couldn’t turn back, since the bus had no back pointer. It has since been upgraded to a doubly linked list. Pittsburgh is still waiting.

Wyrd planned the route with Dijkstra’s shortest-path algorithm, which worked perfectly until a roadie added a road with a negative weight and the band arrived in Ohio before they left.

Nyble wrote every setlist as a queue. In Cleveland, Bytes read it as a stack, and the band opened with the encore. The crowd loved it so much they did it again on purpose the next time through, and you can hear it on this record.

Hotel rooms were assigned by hashing names. In Hashville, Byt and Bytes landed in the same room. The collision was resolved by chaining, and Byt slept in the bathtub.

Every show sold out except one, where the venue’s booking system threw an exception that nobody caught. Every night before going on, the band still walked Null through the setlist, song by song. Wyrd started doing it in thirty-two-bit stereo. The duck has still not commented, but rubber ducks outsold T-shirts at the merch table three to one.

## 1989: Q.E.D.

*1989 progressive metal concept album. Mathematical and statistical foundations.*

Q.E.D. is the 1989 progressive metal concept album from Procedural Byts, and it is one long proof. In 1988 Byt flipped mid-solo and left the band, leaving a single line written on the bottom of the duck: if n, then n plus one. No base case. Nyble sings lead and plays bass, new member Qword plays shred lead guitar, Wyrd plays keyboards and orchestral synths, and Bytes plays drums and sings backup. Twelve songs cover the math of computing: logic, quantifiers, proof by contradiction, induction, sets, functions, the pigeonhole principle, counting, probability, modular arithmetic and graphs, until the band finds the base case in the first song they ever played. Then the duck quacks. Play loud. Show your work.

### The Proof

In 1988, in the middle of a solo, Byt flipped. One bit, one night, from one to zero. He walked off stage and never came back. The band blames a cosmic ray. Wyrd remembers the exact bar he stopped on and has never told anyone which one.

The next morning, Nyble turned Null over and found a single line written on the bottom of the duck, in Byt’s handwriting: “If n, then n plus one.” Just the inductive step. No base case. On its own, it proves nothing.

The band auditioned sixty-three guitarists. The sixty-fourth was Qword: sixty-four bits wide, the biggest member the band has ever had, and painfully aware of replacing the smallest. Qword can play a thousand notes a minute. The fans still chanted for the one note Byt used to play. Qword learned it anyway.

Bytes suggested it might be time for a new name. Nyble refused. The band was called Procedural Byts before Byt flipped, and it would be called Procedural Byts long after. It is, and it always will be.

So they went into the studio to finish Byt’s proof. Long songs, odd time signatures, Wyrd’s synths gone fully orchestral. They searched every set, every function, every graph and every clock. It took eleven songs to realize the base case had been behind them the whole time: the first song they ever played together.

When the proof closed, on the last take of the last song, Null made a sound. A single quack. It is the first value the duck has ever returned.

## 1992: Third Normal Form

*1992 grunge and alternative metal. Data management.*

Third Normal Form is the 1992 album from Procedural Byts: twelve tracks of grunge and alternative metal about how data is stored, linked and kept true. Nyble sings lead and plays bass, Qword plays lead guitar, Wyrd unplugs the synths for a distorted church-sale organ, and Bytes plays drums and sings backup. SQL queries, entity-relationship design, primary and foreign keys, joins, aggregation, update anomalies, normalization, NULL, indexes, transactions and crash recovery. Written after a tour where every member kept their own copy of the setlist and no two ever matched. Null finally gets a song of its own. Play loud. Store it once.

### The Setlist Problem

By 1992, nobody wanted synthesizers or shred solos anymore. Wyrd unplugged the entire synth rig, bought an old organ at a church sale, and played it through an amp with a broken speaker. Qword was told that guitar solos were over and spent the year learning to play three notes, slowly, with feeling.

On the Q.E.D. tour, every member had kept their own copy of the setlist. Nyble’s had the songs in one order and Qword’s in another. Wyrd had renamed two of the songs. Bytes’s copy still listed songs from 1979. When Nyble changed a song, only her copy changed, and every night somebody played the wrong one.

It got worse. They couldn’t add a new song to the set until it had a show to be played at. When they dropped a song, Bytes erased it from his copy, along with the only record of what key it was in.

So they made an album about fixing it. One setlist, kept in one place, with every song stored once and given a number. Every other list refers to the songs by number, so if you change a song, you change it once.

Null got a song of its own, and the band asked the duck the obvious question: are you equal to yourself? The duck said nothing. According to the database, that is the correct answer.

The last thing they recorded was Commit. Afterwards they threw three of the four setlists in the bin and kept one. Nobody has played the wrong song since.

## 1995: Merge Conflict

*1995 industrial metal. Software engineering.*

Merge Conflict is the 1995 album from Procedural Byts: twelve tracks of industrial metal about how software actually gets built, by people, together. Nyble sings lead and plays bass, Qword plays lead guitar, Wyrd plugs the synths back in alongside a rack of samplers, and Bytes plays drums over machine loops and sings backup. Requirements, process, design, patterns, testing, code review, refactoring, technical debt, estimation and version control. The first album the band made with a written plan. Each member recorded on their own branch: the recording took two months, and the merge took four. Play loud. Review everything.

### Four Branches

For the first time in twenty-five years, the band wrote down what they were going to make before they made it. Nyble sent a survey to the fan club asking what people wanted from the next album. The top four answers were louder, longer, angrier and more duck.

Industrial was in, so Wyrd plugged the synthesizers back in and added a rack of samplers. Wyrd recorded the studio’s air conditioner, the photocopier and Bytes dropping his sticks, and turned all three into rhythm tracks.

They tried the waterfall first: a month of requirements, a month of design, then recording, with testing saved for the end. At the end, they discovered the whole album was in the wrong key, and went back to the top.

So each member took their own branch: one copy of the songs each, changed independently for six weeks. The recording took two months. The merge took four. Nyble and Qword had both rewritten the chorus of Merge Conflict on the same night, in completely different ways, and you can hear both on the record.

Every part had to pass code review by another member before it went on the master tape. Qword’s reviews were long and thoughtful. Wyrd’s were precise. Every review Bytes wrote said “louder.” When two members couldn’t agree, they explained their sides to Null, and by the time they finished explaining, one of them usually realized they were wrong.

The album came in eleven weeks late. Bytes suggested bringing in session players to catch up. Nyble pointed out that adding people to a late project makes it later. They did it anyway, and proved her right.

## 1998: Buffer Overflow

*1998 industrial metal with techno crossover. Security.*

Buffer Overflow is the 1998 album from Procedural Byts: twelve tracks of industrial metal crossed with techno about keeping systems and people safe. Nyble sings lead and plays bass, Qword plays lead guitar, Wyrd brings sequencers and acid synths, and Bytes plays live drums over programmed breakbeats and sings backup. The security triad, threat models, passwords and two-factor authentication, least privilege, buffer overflows, input validation, encryption, public keys, digital signatures, man-in-the-middle attacks and social engineering. Written after a fan walked backstage by saying “I’m with the duck.” Play loud. Trust no input.

### I’m With the Duck

By 1998, the band was online: a fan site, a mailing list, and one shared email account whose password everyone knew. Wyrd had discovered techno and arrived at rehearsal with a sequencer, an acid bass synth and a drum machine. Bytes played live drums on top of the machine, louder than it, out of principle.

They decided it was time to take security seriously, and started by threat-modeling the tour. What are we protecting? The master tapes, the setlist, the duck. Who wants it? Bootleggers, a rival band, the label. How would they get it? The answer to almost every question was “the duck.”

Bytes’s password was “drums.” Then “drums1.” Then “Drums1!” Nyble sat him down and explained hashing, salting and passphrases. He now has a password nobody knows, including, some days, Bytes.

The master tapes went to the pressing plant in a locked case, with the key sent separately by a second courier. The case arrived first and sat at the plant for a week. For the next shipment, they used a lock anyone could close but only Nyble could open.

On the first night of the tour, a fan walked straight past security by smiling and saying, “I’m with the duck.” It worked. The band considers it their first social-engineering attack. Backstage passes now need two factors: the laminate, and the answer to a question. The question is “what does the duck say?” The answer is nothing.

## 2001: Race Condition

*2001 nu-metal. Parallel and distributed computing.*

Race Condition is the 2001 album from Procedural Byts: twelve tracks of nu-metal about doing many things at once, on one machine or many, plus a hidden thirteenth. Nyble sings lead and plays bass, Qword plays downtuned seven-string guitar, Wyrd trades the keytar for turntables and samples, and Bytes plays drums and raps the backing vocals. Multicore processors, threads and shared memory, locks, barriers, message passing, embarrassingly parallel work, Amdahl’s law, logical clocks, partial failure, consensus and the CAP theorem. Recorded with all four members playing at once in separate rooms, so the title track came out two different ways. They released both. Play loud. Synchronize.

### Two Versions

The new millennium arrived, and with it, nu-metal. Wyrd traded the keytar for a pair of turntables. Qword got a seven-string guitar and was told, again, that guitar solos would not be happening this decade. Bytes discovered he could rap, and nobody has been able to stop him since.

To save studio time, the producer had all four members record at the same moment, in four separate rooms, with no click track and no way to hear each other. Four threads, one shared tape.

It mostly worked. But the title track came out differently depending on whose tape machine started first. When Nyble’s started first, the song resolved. When Bytes’s started first, the chorus came in a bar early and the whole thing collapsed into a crash. The band couldn’t agree which version was the real one, so they released both. The second one is hidden at the end of the album.

On tour, the band traveled in two buses. A snowstorm cut the phone lines between them for two days, and each bus assumed the other had crashed. Neither had. Each bus decided the setlist on its own, and both ended up playing the same city on the same night, at different venues. The band calls it the split-brain tour.

After that, every decision went to a vote. With four members, every vote tied two to two, so they started counting Null as a fifth voter. The duck abstains every time, and somehow that settles it.

## 2004: Rasterize

*2004 metalcore. Graphics and interactive techniques.*

Rasterize is the 2004 album from Procedural Byts: twelve tracks of metalcore about how computers turn numbers into pictures. Nyble sings the clean choruses and plays bass, Qword is finally allowed guitar solos again, Wyrd adds atmospheric synths, and Bytes plays double-kick drums and screams the backing vocals. Bresenham’s line algorithm, RGB color, triangles, matrix transforms, perspective, the graphics pipeline, the z-buffer, Phong lighting, texture mapping, anti-aliasing, ray tracing and animation. Written while Wyrd built the band’s first 3D music video by hand. Null was modeled with twelve triangles, and the fans liked it better. Play loud. Sixty frames a second.

### Twelve Triangles

In 2004, metalcore arrived, and the band changed shape again. Nyble kept singing the clean, soaring choruses. Bytes discovered he could scream, which the rest of the band had suspected for years. And Qword was told, for the first time since 1989, that guitar solos were back. Qword cried, briefly, then played one on nearly every song.

The label wanted a music video, and Wyrd decided to build it in 3D, by hand. The first thing Wyrd ever drew on a computer screen, back in 1985, was a single straight line using Bresenham’s algorithm. So that is where the album starts.

Every member was modeled out of triangles, moved and spun by matrices, lit, textured and rendered. The first render was a disaster. Bytes appeared in front of Nyble, through his own drum kit, because nothing knew which surface was closer. Qword’s guitar had edges like a staircase. Those two bugs became Z-Buffer and Jaggies.

Wyrd ran out of time before modeling Null and built the duck from just twelve triangles. The fans loved it. The low-poly duck has been the band’s logo ever since, and it outsold the real duck at the merch table.

The final shot was ray traced across four computers and took three weeks, which the band only knew how to split up because of what they learned in 2001. The duck rendered in under a second.

## 2007: User Error

*2007 alternative metal. Human-computer interaction.*

User Error is the 2007 album from Procedural Byts: twelve tracks of alternative metal about designing technology for the people who actually use it. Nyble sings lead and plays bass, Qword plays lead guitar, Wyrd moves to piano and atmospheric synths, and Bytes plays drums and sings backup. Affordances, mental models, feedback, user research, paper prototypes, think-aloud testing, Fitts’s law, memory limits, accessibility, dark patterns and undo. The title is ironic: the album argues there is no such thing as user error, only design error. The first pressing came in packaging nobody could open, which the band admits proved the point. Play loud. Watch your users.

### No Such Thing

In 2007 the band redesigned its fan site, and nobody could find the tour dates. Bytes said it was user error. Nyble said there is no such thing: if a fan can’t find the tour dates, the site failed the fan. The argument lasted a week, and it became an album.

Before writing a single song, the band spent a week answering fan mail by phone, just to hear how people actually used the site. Then Wyrd redrew the whole thing on index cards and tested it with fans before anyone wrote a line of code.

They ran a real usability test, too. Five fans were asked to buy a concert ticket while saying everything they were thinking. All five failed. One bought a T-shirt by accident. Two found the duck page and stayed there. Null sat beside every participant during the test, and several said it made them feel less judged, which has always been the duck’s whole job.

Then a fan wrote to say the site read the tour dates to her screen reader as “image, image, image.” The band rebuilt the site so every image had words, every video had captions, and everything worked from a keyboard. Accessible to All is for her.

The first pressing of the album came in a clever fold-out case that nobody could figure out how to open. Fans sent it back. The band admits it proved the point better than any song on the record. The second pressing has a tab that says “pull here.”

## 2010: Cross-Platform

*2010 electronic rock. Specialized platform development.*

Cross-Platform is the 2010 album from Procedural Byts: twelve tracks of electronic rock about building software for the web, phones, games and tiny embedded devices. Nyble sings lead and plays bass, Qword plays lead guitar, Wyrd drives everything with arpeggiated synths and beats, and Bytes plays drums and electronic pads and sings backup. Clients and servers, HTML, CSS and JavaScript, responsive design, web APIs, the app lifecycle, touch input, battery life, the game loop, entity component systems, embedded systems and the internet of things. Released first as an app. Qword’s solos didn’t fit on a phone screen, and Wyrd made a version that runs on a smart fridge. Play loud. Charge first.

### Released as an App

By 2010, everyone had a phone in their pocket, so the band decided to release the album first as an app. It worked on one kind of phone. Then they made it work on the other kind. Then on the web. Then on tablets, which nobody had planned for.

Qword’s solos didn’t fit on a phone screen. The app scrolled sideways during the long ones, and fans kept pausing them by accident with their thumbs. Qword now plays a compact version for small screens and refuses to call it shorter.

The first version of the app kept the GPS switched on so it could show you the nearest record store. It drained a full battery in an hour. The fix became Battery Low. Getting the app to pick up exactly where it left off after a phone call took another month, and became Background.

Bytes wanted a game in the app, so Wyrd built one: catch the duck. Every object in it was an entity made of components. Null got a position, a sprite and a velocity, but no sound component. Nobody added one. Nobody needed to.

Then Wyrd made a version that runs on a smart fridge, in sixty-four kilobytes of memory. It plays the album whenever you open the door. It is, by a wide margin, the most-played version.

After the four-rooms recording of 2001, the band recorded this one the old way: all four in one room, on one platform. Everyone agreed it was a relief.

## 2013: Cache Me If You Can

*2013 djent progressive metal. Systems fundamentals.*

Cache Me If You Can is the 2013 album from Procedural Byts: twelve tracks of djent about the ideas that run through every layer of a computer system. Nyble sings lead and plays bass, Qword moves to an eight-string guitar, Wyrd lays down ambient synths, and Bytes plays polyrhythmic drums and growls the backing vocals. Caching, latency and bandwidth, bottlenecks, queues, state machines, leaky abstractions, virtual machines, containers, fault tolerance, availability, and the two hard things in computer science. The band toured as four virtual machines on one very loud server, until its fan failed and took everyone down at once. Play loud. Measure first.

### One Very Loud Host

By 2013, the band’s tour rig weighed four tons. So they virtualized it: one enormous server on stage, running four virtual machines, one for each member’s gear. The label called it more reliable. Qword, meanwhile, moved to an eight-string guitar and insists that eight strings and eight bits in a byte is a coincidence.

On the first night, the server’s fan failed during the second song and all four virtual machines went down at once, in front of the whole crowd. Four machines, one point of failure. After that, there were two servers. That night became Fault Tolerant and Five Nines.

In stadiums, the sound from the far speakers arrived a quarter of a second late. Bytes started playing along with the echo instead of the beat, and the whole show slowed to a crawl until Nyble stamped the tempo back. That became Latency.

Halfway through the tour, Wyrd moved the synths out of a virtual machine and into a container. It started in one second. Everyone else’s took a minute. Wyrd has not stopped mentioning it.

The band cached the setlist on every member’s phone so nobody would need a signal backstage. Then Nyble changed a song, and three of the four cached copies kept the old one for a week. It was the four-setlist problem from 1992 all over again, and it became the closer, Two Hard Things.

Through all of it, one component of the rig never failed: Null. The duck has had perfect uptime since 1970, mostly by not doing anything.

## 2016: Terms of Service

*2016 post-metal. Society, ethics, and professionalism.*

Terms of Service is the 2016 album from Procedural Byts: twelve tracks of post-metal about what computing does to people, and what the people who build it owe everyone else. Nyble sings lead and plays bass, Qword plays layered, delay-drenched guitar, Wyrd builds atmospheric drones, and Bytes plays slow, crushing drums and shouts the backing vocals from a distance. Terms of service, privacy, copyright and open source, safety-critical failure, whistleblowing, codes of ethics, ethical frameworks, equity, the environmental cost of computing, automation, and data protection law. Written after the band read its 1970 record contract for the first time and found it had signed away the rights to the duck. Play loud. Read the fine print.

### Page Eighty-Seven

In 2016, a student writing a paper about the band asked who owned the rights to their songs. Nobody knew. So, for the first time in forty-six years, the band read its 1970 record contract. All 112 pages of it.

Page eighty-seven said the label owned “the likeness of any mascot, waterfowl or technical advisor associated with the Artist.” They had signed away the rights to Null in 1970. Every duck on every T-shirt since had paid the label a royalty.

Then they read the terms of service for their own website, app and fridge. Their app had been sending fans’ locations to an advertising library nobody remembered adding. Wyrd found it. Bytes admitted he had clicked “I agree” on the band’s behalf about four thousand times and had read none of them.

Post-metal suited the mood: long, quiet stretches for thinking, and then a wall of sound. Wyrd built drones. Qword ran every guitar part through six delay pedals. Duty of Care is the most serious song the band has ever recorded, and they decided it would have no jokes in it.

They spent the album fixing what they could. They deleted fan data they didn’t need, rewrote their terms in plain language, and published their website code under an open license. Strangers fixed three bugs in the first week.

Finally, they renegotiated the 1970 contract and got the duck back. Null’s likeness is now released under a Creative Commons license. Attribution required. The duck has never commented on legal matters.

## 2019: Gradient Descent

*2019 synthwave metal. Artificial intelligence.*

Gradient Descent is the 2019 album from Procedural Byts: twelve tracks of synthwave metal about how machines learn. Nyble sings lead and plays bass, Qword plays neon shred guitar, Wyrd goes back to analog synths from the 1980s, and Bytes plays big gated-reverb drums over drum machines and sings backup. The Turing test, search and A*, minimax, expert systems, Bayes, training data, neural networks, gradient descent, overfitting, clustering, reinforcement learning and generative models. Written after the label trained an AI on every song the band ever recorded and released it as Procedural Bots. It topped the charts for a week, until fans noticed every song had a duck with five legs. Play loud. Count the legs.

### Procedural Bots

In 2019, without asking, the band’s label fed every song Procedural Byts had ever recorded into a neural network and released the result as an album by a new band: Procedural Bots. It went straight to the top of the charts.

It lasted a week. Then fans noticed that every Procedural Bots song had a duck in it, and every duck had five legs. The model had learned that Procedural Byts songs always have a duck. It had never learned how many legs a duck has.

There were other tells. The training data included Bytes’s practice tapes, so the AI band always came in a beat early. And because it had only ever heard this band, it believed every song in the world was about computer science.

The one member it could never imitate was Null. The AI kept generating a duck that quacked. Fans who had listened since 1989 knew the duck had quacked exactly once, and that’s how they could always tell.

So the band made an album about how machines actually learn, to understand what had happened to them. Synthwave was the obvious sound for a story about machines from the future built out of the past: Wyrd went back to analog synthesizers from the 1980s, Bytes got his gated-reverb drums back, and Qword’s solos turned neon.

At the release show, the band played fans a blind test, one song by Procedural Byts and one by Procedural Bots. Most picked correctly. Several picked the wrong one on purpose, because the five-legged duck was funnier.

## 2024: Superposition

*genre-blending metal. Bonus: quantum computing.*

Superposition is the 2024 reunion album from Procedural Byts: thirteen tracks that each exist in two eras at once, from 1970 proto-metal to 2019 synthwave. Byt is back, as a qubit: both in the band and not, until someone checks. Nyble sings lead and plays bass, Qword and Byt trade lead guitar across fifty years, Wyrd plays synths, and Bytes plays drums and sings backup. Qubits, superposition, measurement, interference, quantum gates, entanglement, no-cloning, Grover’s search, Shor’s algorithm, quantum key distribution, decoherence, and what quantum computers can and can’t do. It ends with Hello, World, the first song they ever played. Play loud. Don’t look.

### Both and Neither

In 2024, a package arrived at the studio with no return address. Inside was a single guitar pick, painted 0 on one side and 1 on the other. That night, at rehearsal, Byt was there. And he wasn’t. Both, until anyone looked directly at him.

In 1970, Byt had been a bit: on or off. In 1988, he flipped. Now he was a qubit, in both states at once until measured. Every time someone checked whether he was really back in the band, the answer came out yes or no at random. After a week, the band stopped checking.

Wyrd remembered the exact bar Byt had stopped on in 1988 and had never told anyone which one it was. At the first rehearsal, Byt picked up on the very next bar.

Because the band was now in a superposition of its whole history, so was the album. Every song exists in two eras at once, from 1970 proto-metal to 2019 synthwave, and Qword and Byt trade lead guitar across fifty years. Every take existed in two versions until the engineer listened to it, and then only one. The engineer eventually asked everyone to stop looking at the tape.

Bytes tried to make a backup copy of Byt, in case he disappeared again. It can’t be done.

Nyble had said in 1989 that the band would always be called Procedural Byts. Now nobody can prove she was wrong.

And the duck? Null quacked exactly once, in 1989. On this album, in superposition, it has both quacked and not quacked on every song. The record ends before anyone checks.
